What is Civic Education?

Civic Education helps us become informed, responsible and active members of society.

  • Learn about rights, duties, rules and public decisions.
  • Build skills for listening, solving problems and working together.
  • Practise values such as honesty, respect and fairness.
  • Use what you learn to improve life in your community.
Picture this: Your class needs a monitor. Instead of choosing the loudest person, pupils discuss the role, hear each candidate and make a fair choice.

Part 3 of 7 · Competence 1.1.1.1

Five areas of everyday life

Civic Education connects many parts of life. These areas can overlap.

Social

How people live together.

Example: including a new pupil.

Political

Leadership and public decisions.

Example: discussing how representatives are chosen.

Economic

Work, money and resources.

Example: planning how to use a club's funds.

Cultural

Languages, traditions and ways of life.

Example: learning about a ceremony respectfully.

Environmental

Our surroundings and natural resources.

Example: reducing litter around school.
Part 4 of 7 · Competence 1.1.1.1

Rights and responsibilities

  • A right is an entitlement or freedom a person should enjoy.
  • A duty is something a person is required or expected to do.
  • A responsibility is something we should take care of or do in a responsible way.
At school: Education is a right. Looking after shared books and respecting other learners helps everyone learn. A child's rights do not disappear because the child makes a mistake.

Governance concerns how decisions are made and how people and resources are managed. Civic Education helps us ask whether decisions are fair and leaders are accountable.

Part 5 of 7 · Competence 1.1.1.1

Make a thoughtful choice

During a discussion, Mwila disagrees with Chanda. What should Mwila do?

Compare two possible responses

“Keep quiet; your idea is useless.” This shuts someone out.

“I see it differently. Can we compare our reasons?” This shows respect and critical thinking.

  • Respect: treat others with dignity.
  • Tolerance: allow different views without insulting people.
  • Honesty: tell the truth and use resources properly.
  • Fairness: give people a reasonable chance to take part.
Part 6 of 7 · Competence 1.1.1.2

Turn an idea into civic action

Civic participation means taking part in activities that support the wellbeing of a group or community.

  1. Notice: identify a real problem, such as litter near classrooms.
  2. Listen: ask learners and staff what would help.
  3. Plan: agree on a safe task, permission, tools and roles.
  4. Act: work together with suitable adult supervision.
  5. Reflect: explain what changed and what to improve.
Choose one: help with a school activity, join a student council discussion, or suggest a way to care for shared books. You do not need to be an adult voter to contribute.

For clean-ups, leave sharp objects, chemicals and unknown waste to responsible adults. Do not confront someone suspected of wrongdoing; use a trusted teacher or appropriate reporting channel.

Part 7 of 7 · Competence 1.1.1.2

Your small community project

Choose a school or community issue and write a short plan. You can discuss it with your teacher or guardian.

Your plan stays on this browser. Avoid names or private information.

See an example and check your plan

Our shared books are hard to find. With our teacher's permission, we will label a shelf and agree on a borrowing list. Class volunteers will help. After one week, we will check whether fewer books are missing.

  • Is the problem clear?
  • Are the roles and actions safe and realistic?
  • Does the plan include others fairly?
  • Can you explain how you will check the result?

This is an example, not the only correct answer. Writing a plan does not prove you completed the activity.
